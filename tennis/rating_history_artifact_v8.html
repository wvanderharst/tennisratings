How the chart works
New in October 2026: every player now has two ratings, one for serve and one for return (each with its own hard, clay and grass version: the general rating plus a surface gap, where the gaps balance out at hard 60% / clay 30% / grass 10%, so the general line always sits between a player's best and worst surface). The line shows their average, which is what decides most matches; the Serve and Return buttons show each half on its own, and Raw ratings plots the numbers themselves instead of win-odds (0 = an average tour player; the general rating is (serve + return) / 2; the same numbers appear in the Current ratings tab). Closing (the set-level finishing rating) and clutch (the deciding-set rating, with the weight the match odds use) are on by default, so the line shows the full player as the odds see them. The model predicts a match the way it is played: each player's chance of winning a point on serve comes from their serve rating against the opponent's return rating, then holds, sets (tiebreaks point by point) and the match follow. It also learns faster from a player's first matches and no longer lets ratings fade between matches. Held-out logloss improved by 0.0017 (men) and 0.0025 (women), the largest single step in a long while; most of it comes from the point→hold→set prediction rather than from the split itself. Ratings sit on a wider scale than before, so compare lines with each other, not with old screenshots. vs No. 5–15 shows Strength, as in the Current ratings tab: the average win chance against the players ranked 5th to 15th at that moment in history. The Serve and Return buttons show one half at a time: the win chance against the reference opponent if the player's other half were exactly at the reference level — a big server's Serve line sits far above their Return line. The history below describes how the rating evolved before this change. The line is each player's rating converted into expected win-probability (log-odds) against a reference opponent — whoever was ranked 10th among active tour players (30+ matches, played in the last year) at that exact point in history, not a fixed level pinned to the present — so 1978's line is measured against 1978's top 10, not today's. It still runs through the same game→set→match→calibration chain either way, so it stays smooth across the calendar rather than zig-zagging with the tournament schedule, and reads on a scale where 0 means roughly a coin flip against that era's 10th-best active player. Ringed bubbles mark Slam title wins. Surface-adjusted (off by default) swaps the line for a fixed blend of general skill and that player's independently-tracked surface-specific rating on whichever surface that snapshot's match was actually played on (once they have enough matches on it). Two earlier ways of splitting credit between general and surface skill were tried and set aside: a version that decided, match by match and in real time, how much of each result counted as surface skill versus general skill smoothed the line nicely but cost real predictive accuracy, because that split has to guess — before it's known whether a hot streak will last — how much of it is general improvement rather than a surface-specific spike; and a version that scored general and surface skill fully independently (each against its own separate, partial prediction) was more accurate but sawtoothed visibly with the clay season for surface-lopsided players. The version live now scores each match once, against the same combined blend the model actually predicts with, and then updates both general and surface skill by the full resulting surprise — nothing is ever split or reallocated between them. That combined prediction is simply a better signal than either piece alone, so both general and surface skill get cleaner updates: on held-out testing this improved both logloss and Brier score (the two measures used to validate everything else on this page) over the independent version, and cut a specialist's within-year swing by a meaningful fraction on its own, as a side effect of the cleaner signal rather than a deliberate smoothing trade. You may still see a player's general-rating line rise during a strong surface season and ease back afterward — that reactivity is real predictive signal, just less noisy than before. Players under 24 also get a faster-reacting version of this same update (1.4× the normal step size) — testing found the model otherwise lags genuine, rapid early-career improvement, since a fixed step size has to compromise between tracking a 21-year-old's real jump in form and not over-reacting to a 31-year-old's noisy week; the same idea tried in the other direction, reacting faster to older players on the theory that decline should be easier to catch quickly, was tested just as rigorously and consistently made both accuracy and calibration worse, so it was left out. New players now start slightly below tour-average rather than exactly at it (a little lower if their first match is at challenger level) — testing found the model had been systematically treating newcomers as average players when most are well below that, and fixing the starting point was worth more held-out accuracy than any other single change. Separately, the live win-odds (not this chart, since they describe the match rather than the player) now also correct for rest and match rhythm, home crowd, qualifier/wildcard entry, a recent retirement or withdrawal, and height on fast courts; together with the new starting point that took held-out accuracy from 64.3% to 65.2%. Ratings now also move about 20% less per match than before: once newcomers start nearer their real level, a smaller step tracks true form better (checked on held-out matches). Updating after every set instead of after every match was tested too and made no difference, since the per-set surprises add up to the same thing by the next match. Since 1991, where the match stats exist, ratings now learn from points won rather than games won — a much finer-grained result, so a tight 7-6 7-6 and a comfortable 6-3 6-3 are told apart properly. New players with an ATP ranking start at a level set by that ranking rather than one flat starting point, and ratings drift with age between matches (young players rising a little, players past 30 easing down), so a layoff no longer freezes a 21-year-old or a 34-year-old in place. Together these took held-out accuracy to 65.5%. General skill (and surface rating, and closing) does decay gradually during long layoffs (half-life ~3.8 years, past a 30-day grace period), so a retired or injured player doesn't sit frozen at their career-peak rating forever. Stable general rating (off by default) shows a second, independent line purely for display: a running average of that same general rating over time. Because it never has to decide which bucket a match's surprise belongs to — it just smooths the one number the model already settled on — turning it on costs nothing, since (like surface-adjusted) it never feeds back into the model that computes anyone's actual win-odds, closing, or head-to-head adjustment. It's mutually exclusive with surface-adjusted. Closing adjustment runs each player's tight-set "clutch" correction through the same game→set→match conversion the live model uses, rather than adding it on directly — closing is a set-level effect and general skill a game-level one, so naive addition isn't valid. vs Top 10/20/50 switches which rank the line (and the win-odds) are measured against — a tougher or easier bar depending on whether it's the 10th-, 20th-, or 50th-best active player of the moment — and now combines freely with every other toggle on this page: Surface-adjusted, Stable, the surface picker, and Closing all work against any of the three references, with the browser running the same calibration chain live instead of everything being precomputed against one fixed reference. The General / Hard / Clay / Grass picker is a different view again: instead of blending general skill into the surface a match happened to be played on, it shows the same blend pinned to ONE explicit surface, continuously, regardless of what surface a player is actually playing at that moment — useful for seeing how a clay specialist's clay game and hard-court game have each moved on their own terms, including surfaces they rarely play. (An earlier version of this toggle showed pure, unblended surface skill with no general skill mixed in at all — it looked misleadingly negative for even the greatest players on their best surface, because that pure number only accumulates from matches actually played on that surface, a small fraction of any career, while general skill accumulates from every match; comparing an under-sampled number to a full-career reference produced a large, meaningless gap that had nothing to do with real surface ability. Blending general skill back in, at the same weight used everywhere else, fixes it.) Surface-adjusted, Stable general rating, and the surface picker remain mutually exclusive with each other (only one line at a time), but that's now a display choice, not a limitation of what's computable. Switch the x-axis to Age or Years on tour to line up different eras on equal footing. Hover a line, bubble, or pill to isolate it. Drag across the chart to zoom into a period (both axes rescale to what's in view, and each line switches from monthly to weekly snapshots once that player's monthly points would otherwise be sparse dots rather than a curve); use "Reset zoom" to zoom back out. Every player with at least 40 tour-level matches is searchable below, not just Grand Slam finalists — the weekly zoom detail is kept for the ~300 most active players to keep the page a reasonable size, everyone else still gets the full monthly trajectory and title history.

Every match of the player, newest first. Win chance is the full model's pre-match probability (ratings, closing, head-to-head and the corrections). The Δ columns are what that match did to the player's ratings: overall level (the average of serve and return), serve, return, and the level on that match's surface (general plus the surface gap). Ratings move on points won on serve and on return compared with what the model expected against that opponent, so a win can lower a rating (winning while being outplayed on points) and a loss can raise it. Before 1991, and wherever serve stats are missing, ratings learn from games won instead. In a retirement with match stats, only the player who did not retire learns from the points played (the injured player's points mostly show the injury); walkovers and defaults don't change the ratings. Win chance is shown for completed matches only. Ratings are in the model's units: 0 is an average tour player. Click an opponent to open their profile. Predicted titles add up the player's title chance on the eve of every event they entered: each entrant's ratings frozen before the event, then 1,200 (Slams 3,000) seeded random draws played out with the model (ratings, holds and sets, closing; best of five where played; and the observed retirement/walkover risk per match, in which case the result is close to a coin flip). Counted: tour-level knockout events (men 1968 on, women 1990 on); not counted: Davis/Fed Cup, round-robin season finals and men's challengers. Won more than predicted = beat the odds.
